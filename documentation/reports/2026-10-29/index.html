02

Summary#

This week in one paragraph

A. Adiga asked for visual inspection, a per-location breakdown, a temporal summary, and a viewer for any location and date. All four are done. Every configuration was re-run from all 35 weeks of the test season, so any date can be plotted, and the forecasts are in the repository as readable tables. The plots show what the pooled score hid: peak emphasis helps during the surge and draws a V after the turn, with the 3- and 4-week median climbing again while the data falls. Baseline does this too, emphasis about twice as often. Because each horizon has its own model, emphasis can be confined to 1-2 weeks; that turns the best emphasis arm from a loss into a gain in 40 of 52 locations, but it was chosen on this season. Inspection also found a problem no configuration fixes: after their peak, the largest states are forecast to collapse when they did not.

Visual inspection#

Seven states (California, Florida, Texas, New York, Pennsylvania, plus North Carolina and Ohio, where no configuration helps), forecast dates Nov 29 - Feb 7, five configurations each. Every claim below was checked against the forecast files. Each "see" link opens the viewer at that state and date. Mechanisms marked inferred are hypotheses, not tested.

The season splits into three phases, set by each state's own curve rather than the calendar.

PhaseWhat the plots showBest configuration
Surge (still rising; Nov 29 - Dec 20 in most states)Every configuration forecasts too low: the observation is above baseline's 90% band in 26% of rows and below it in none. Peak emphasis steepens 3-4 weeks and lands closer.peak oversample 5x, peak weight 3x
Turn (the 1-3 forecast dates around the state's maximum)The 1-week forecast usually turns down, often too far. The failure is at 3-4 weeks: the median dips, then climbs again while the data falls (a V). Peak emphasis makes it larger and more frequent.baseline, peak oversample 2x
After the turn (Jan 17 - Feb 7)Small and mid-size states are fine. In the largest states every configuration forecasts a further steep fall while the curve held or rose again.none

1. After the turn: a V at 3-4 weeks

problem

The 1-week median usually falls with the curve. The 3- and 4-week medians climb again. Baseline does this in 14% of forecasts made right after a falling week, peak emphasis in 26-35%. This is the 1-2 week gain and 3-4 week loss from round 2. Inferred: the 3-4 week models lean on the season ("January means high") more than on recent momentum. See Pennsylvania, Jan 10, Ohio, Jan 3.

2. Bad dates follow each state's own turn

finding

Pooled, peak emphasis is worst on Jan 3 and Jan 10, when the northeast and Ohio turned. Texas turned on Dec 27, so its bad dates are Dec 20 - Jan 3, and on Jan 10 peak emphasis is better there (WIS 129-138 vs 212). See Texas, Dec 20, Texas, Jan 10.

3. Large states collapse after the peak

problem

Above 1,500 admissions, the 1-week median is 0.65 of the last value while the observed value is 0.83 (31 forecasts, Dec 27 - Feb 21); below 1,000 the two agree. 15 of the 18 forecasts that halve the last value are in the five largest states. No tested configuration fixes it. Inferred: a milder form of the scale ceiling that breaks the US forecast. See Florida, Jan 24 (684 vs 1,606, then a second peak of 2,515), Texas, Jan 17 (517 vs 1,388).

4. Some large-state gain is not skill

caveat

California's gain from peak emphasis comes largely from Jan 10-24, when its rebound happened to match a real second rise. In Texas and Florida part of the gain comes from Jan 24-31, when every configuration was too low and a higher forecast was less wrong. See California, Jan 17.

5. Intervals misplaced, not too wide

problem

90% coverage over these weeks is 0.43-0.82 per state, against the 0.90 target. The misses are one-sided: the truth exits the top of the band in the surge and the bottom after the turn. The 50% band covers only 0.23-0.48. See Florida, Dec 13.

6. 52-week window fails at the peak

problem

It flat-lines during every surge and crashes after the turn (California, Jan 17: about 300 against 1,650-2,150 observed). It wins only on Jan 10 in New York, North Carolina and Ohio, where forecasting low happened to be right. See New York, Dec 6.

Which configuration to trust when
  • While a state is still rising: peak oversample 5x or peak weight 3x for 2-4 weeks; all configurations still under-predict, so read the upper band.
  • From the first falling week: baseline or peak oversample 2x for 1-2 weeks; discount every configuration's 3-4 week forecast (the V).
  • After the peak in a large state: none; all of them forecast a collapse.
  • Peak oversample 2x is the safest single choice: most of the surge gain in the large states, the smallest penalty at the turn.

Limits

One seed. 22 of the 77 figures were studied closely, the rest through the forecast files. Phase boundaries were read from each curve by eye. The inferred mechanisms could be tested with feature attributions for the 4-week model on Jan 3-10 and a count of training targets above 1,500.

Results in brief#

XGBoost, 2025-26 season, 35 weekly forecast dates, seed 42, US excluded from scoring.

  • Direction. Baseline's median points the right way (up, flat or down) in 72.5% of rows; always saying "flat" would get 39.3%. Only 53.8% in the peak phase. It says "up" when the curve fell in 17% of peak rows; peak emphasis 19-23%, the 52-week window 33%.
  • Combining configurations. Each horizon has its own model, so peak emphasis can be used for 1-2 weeks only, without retraining.

Confining peak emphasis to the 1- and 2-week models keeps most of the surge gain without the V.

Caveats

Every rule above was designed after looking at this season, so the gains are in-sample and small (about 2-3% of peak WIS) on one seed. The calendar rule wins only because Dec 31 fell at this season's turn; the curve rule alone changes sign across reasonable thresholds. The horizon rule, alone or with the curve rule, stays a gain in every setting tried. A held-out season is needed before adopting any of them.

Open questions#

Switch to FluSight's 23 quantiles?

The model emits 5 quantiles, so the plots show a 90% band rather than 95%, and WIS is not on the FluSight scale (differences between configurations are still valid). Switching means retraining round 2 and the weekly run, about 9 hours of compute, and changes every WIS number, so it is best decided before the paper's results are written.

Test the horizon rule on a held-out season?

Proposal: fix the horizon rule as the primary hypothesis and horizon + curve as secondary, then score them on 2024-25 with 3 seeds (about 2.5 hours). This is the test that would make the result reportable.

Why do the largest states collapse after their peak?

A hypothesis, not tested: a milder form of the scale ceiling that breaks the US forecast (the largest training value is 4,729, Texas). Counting training targets above 1,500 and looking at feature attributions for those forecasts would test it.

Is the US forecast worth fixing?

The model trains on state rows and cannot reach national counts (about 21,000 at the peak); its 1-week US forecast collapses to the 30% floor with all quantiles equal. Options: model the US separately, or aggregate state forecasts.

How should "flat" be defined for direction?

Currently a change under 10% of the last value or 10 admissions, whichever is larger. If the lab uses FluSight's rate-trend categories, the metric should follow them.

Still open from earlier

October as off-season or growth; which in-house model to onboard first and who can grant code access.
